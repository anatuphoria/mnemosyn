## Overview
Light falling on the retina is turned into signals that run through the optic nerve, partly cross at the optic chiasm, relay in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and reach the primary visual cortex around the calcarine sulcus. Each hemisphere sees the opposite half of the visual field [purves2018].

## Step 1
Role: Carries the output of the retinal ganglion cells of the {same} eye to the optic chiasm [purves2018].
Lesion: Blindness of the {same} eye, with loss of its direct pupillary light reflex [kandel2021].

## Step 2
Role: Fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross here, so each optic tract carries the opposite half of the visual field from both eyes; fibres from the temporal half of the {same} retina stay on the {same} side [purves2018].
Lesion: A midline lesion here, for example a pituitary tumour, cuts the crossing fibres and takes both temporal visual fields (bitemporal hemianopia) [kandel2021].

## Step 3
Role: The {same} lateral geniculate nucleus relays the {opposite} half of the visual field from both eyes, keeping each eye in separate layers [purves2018].
Lesion: Loss of the {opposite} half of the visual field in both eyes ({opposite} homonymous hemianopia) [kandel2021].

## Step 4
Role: Carries visual signals from the {same} lateral geniculate nucleus to the {same} visual cortex; fibres for the upper field loop forward through the temporal lobe (Meyer's loop) [purves2018].
Lesion: Loss of the {opposite} half of the visual field in both eyes; a temporal lobe lesion of Meyer's loop takes mainly the {opposite} upper quarter [kandel2021].

## Step 5
Role: The primary visual cortex maps the {opposite} half of the visual field, with central vision at the occipital pole [purves2018].
Lesion: Loss of the {opposite} half of the visual field in both eyes, often with central vision spared when the occipital pole keeps its blood supply [kandel2021].
