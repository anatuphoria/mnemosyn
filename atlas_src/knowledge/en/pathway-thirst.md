## Overview
When the blood becomes too concentrated, the brain releases vasopressin, which makes the kidneys keep water, and makes the person thirsty, so that plasma osmolality returns to normal [hall2021] [robertson1976].

## Step 1
Role: Osmoreceptors in the lamina terminalis, at the front wall of the third ventricle, sense the osmolality of the blood [hall2021].
Disorders: Damage here can remove both thirst and vasopressin release, causing dangerously high sodium without thirst [christcrain2019].

## Step 2
Role: Large neurons of the supraoptic nucleus make vasopressin and send it down their axons to the posterior pituitary [hall2021].
Disorders: Loss of these neurons, after injury, surgery or tumours, causes central diabetes insipidus with large volumes of dilute urine [christcrain2019].
Drugs: Desmopressin replaces the missing vasopressin in central diabetes insipidus [christcrain2019].

## Step 3
Role: Large neurons of the paraventricular nucleus also make vasopressin for the posterior pituitary [hall2021].
Disorders: Central diabetes insipidus appears only when most vasopressin neurons, here and in the supraoptic nucleus, are lost [christcrain2019].

## Step 4
Role: The posterior pituitary releases vasopressin into the blood as osmolality rises above about 282 mOsm/kg [robertson1976] [baylis1980].
Disorders: Vasopressin released while osmolality is low, from the pituitary or from a tumour, as in the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIADH), keeps water and lowers plasma sodium [hall2021].

## Step 5
Role: Vasopressin acts on the collecting ducts so the kidneys return water to the blood and pass concentrated urine [hall2021].
Disorders: When the kidneys cannot answer vasopressin, in nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, the urine stays dilute even when vasopressin is high [christcrain2019].
