## Overview
The stress axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis) turns a perceived threat into a rise of cortisol within minutes. Cortisol mobilises energy and, through feedback on the brain and pituitary, switches the response off again [ulrichlai2009].

## Step 1
Role: The amygdala drives the axis during threat and becomes more excitable under cortisol [ulrichlai2009].
Disorders: In PTSD the axis is often set unusually, with altered cortisol levels and strong feedback [kandel2021].

## Step 2
Role: Neurons of the paraventricular nucleus release corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) into the pituitary portal blood [ulrichlai2009].
Disorders: Overactive CRH release is found in many people with severe depression [kandel2021].

## Step 3
Role: The anterior pituitary answers CRH by releasing adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into the blood within minutes [ulrichlai2009].
Disorders: An ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma causes Cushing's disease [kandel2021].

## Step 4
Role: ACTH makes the adrenal cortex release cortisol, which peaks about 20 to 40 minutes after a short stressor [dickerson2004].
Disorders: Too much cortisol causes Cushing's syndrome with weight gain, high blood sugar and low mood; too little (Addison's disease) causes weakness and low blood pressure [kandel2021].
Drugs: Glucocorticoid medicines such as prednisolone act like cortisol and, taken for long, suppress the axis [kandel2021].

## Step 5
Role: Cortisol acts on hippocampal receptors, which signal the hypothalamus to end the stress response [ulrichlai2009].
Disorders: Long-term high cortisol may damage the hippocampus and weaken this brake, as reported in chronic stress and depression [kandel2021].

## Step 6
Role: The medial prefrontal and subgenual cingulate cortex also inhibit the axis and help recovery after stress [ulrichlai2009].
Disorders: Weak prefrontal control is linked to prolonged stress responses in depression [kandel2021].
